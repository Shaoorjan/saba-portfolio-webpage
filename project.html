The Crosswalk Project

The U.S. Nonprofit Safeguarding Crosswalk Project

An open-access structural framework bridging international standards and U.S. domestic compliance.

Project overview flyer. A foundational summary outlining the Project’s scope and core objectives. Download (Word, draft)

The project is often shortened to “the PSEA Crosswalk,” after the international standard it maps — the IASC Six Core Principles Relating to Sexual Exploitation and Abuse. The tools themselves use the broader PSEAH framing, which adds harassment, and sit inside safeguarding, the umbrella duty.

The problem

The nonprofit sector operates on an implicit trust to safeguard its beneficiaries, and that trust is not anchored by a legal duty of general application. Module 1 of this project takes the international safeguarding standard — the IASC Six Core Principles — decomposes it into thirteen discrete, testable obligations, and maps each against United States law.

The finding is precise and falsifiable: not one of the thirteen obligations is anchored by a United States requirement of general application that both reaches nonprofits as a class and protects the people they serve. Coverage exists, but it is confined to a certified sub-sector, conditioned on particular federal funding, directed at employees rather than participants, or triggered only after harm. Where a federal requirement does reach an organization, it is candid about how far it goes:

NSCHC requirements are baseline screening procedures. Organizations should institute a holistic framework for safeguarding beneficiaries of service.

— AmeriCorps, National Service Criminal History Check Manual.

The requirement applies to AmeriCorps grantees and subrecipients; the framework it calls for is left to each organization to build.

The sector’s front line is also largely unpaid. In 2023, 75.7 million Americans (28.3 percent) formally volunteered through an organization, against 12.8 million paid nonprofit jobs in 2022, yet United States law allocates protective duties by employment status rather than by the aid relationship itself. The design problem is solved in one certified sub-sector, where a complete operational regime governs Medicare- and Medicaid-certified long-term care facilities. The scope problem is not.

(For the full mapping and citations, see the Module 1 crosswalk PDF and the Research page.)

The method

The project runs a four-step iterative cycle:

  1. Map: Analyzes each obligation of the international standard against U.S. law.
  2. Convert: Translates findings into adoptable, open-access tools.
  3. Collect: Gathers structured feedback from organizations adapting the tools.
  4. Publish: Releases revised versions and new modules incorporating that feedback.

All outputs are released under a Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 license and deposited on Zenodo as versioned records with permanent DOIs (Module 1 crosswalk, 10.5281/zenodo.22022939; toolkit, 10.5281/zenodo.22023886). The work is built to be checked: every row of the crosswalk can be tested against a primary legal source, and documented corrections improve the map rather than undermine it.

How organizations participate

Organizations adopt and adapt the free tools to their own context.

  • Run the self-assessment: Use the 30-indicator self-assessment tool to locate your internal gaps against the mapped obligations.
  • Submit feedback: Complete and send the optional Adopter Feedback Form, which takes 15 to 20 minutes and treats anonymity as the default.
  • Share the toolkit: Distribute the resources through your own networks.

Organizations that find the work useful can say so in writing, and that feedback directly shapes the next published version of each tool. Participation is framed as a research contribution: the more adopters report what worked and what did not, the more accurate and usable each subsequent version becomes.

The roadmap

With all four tools now published (including the Safeguarding Training Outlines), planned further work includes additional crosswalk modules covering ST/SGB sections 4 and 6 in full, the Core Humanitarian Standard, and the Keeping Children Safe standards, together with a fifty-state appendix and revised toolkit editions informed by adopters. A maturity-model methodology module is planned as a later stage.
